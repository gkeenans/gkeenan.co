I’ve been meaning to write something, *anything*, about my experiences over the last, like, five months–holy shit, it’s been nearly five months since I left the US. *Holy shit!* I thought just now, sitting on the couch, wondering what I should do with myself, since my wife is traveling for a few weeks and I have essentially nothing going on that doesn’t involve walking and feeding and cuddling with my dog... 

*Today marks four months in Poland.* 

Exactly four months ago I was boarding a train from Berlin to Warsaw, completely oblivious to the fact that just a few short hours later, the train would break down and leave me and my fellow passengers stranded in the Polish countryside–in a place I would describe as as-middle-of-nowhere-as-you-can-get–baking in the summer heat wave that swept across Europe, before being absolutely dumpstered by a sudden downpour that caused us to be evacuated to a local fire station, away from the dilapidated brick apartment building in which we desperately sought shelter.

And I know what you’re thinking: *I can’t wait to hear about that harrowing adventure!* I know, it’s wild. I still can’t believe it happened.

But that’s not the story I’m here to tell today. Sorry! I’ll write about it at some point. There’s some good stuff there. I just need to reflect on, you know, the bigger picture. The *four months* of it all. 

Four months! That’s a third of a year! And it just... *happened.* That’s how time works now. Months and years just happen. The choices I make seem so significant in the moment, and then I’m dealing with the ramifications of said choices, and then I’m reflecting on them from a distance.

Like, Christ, it’s so charming that back in *March* I had a [mini-meltdown](https://gkeenan.co/avgb/lmao-almost-started-crying-about-polish-7-eleven-this-morning/) worrying about whether or not I’d be able to figure out where to buy groceries once we moved. And now, that’s just a part of the mundanity of everyday life. We found an apartment in about a week[^1] (!), and there’s a grocery store on the ground floor of our building. I can get my lemons (cytryna) and my yogurt (jogurt) and my spinach (szpinak) and my bread (chleb) and, uh, what else do I buy at the grocery sto–OATS (płatki owsiane górskie). I buy oats. I make overnight oats now. They’re nice.

There’s a bakery just around the corner.[^2] There’s a cafe that’s a two minute walk from our front door. There’s a wonderful specialty coffee shop less than ten minutes away. It’s run by an incredibly kind woman named Aga, and her husband, Dawid. Now, rather than having a bag of beans shipped to my door, I get to go and look at packages. I get recommendations from a person in real life. I get to make choices and experiment and learn about what I like, and as a result, my coveted morning ritual has never been in a better place.

Ten minutes in a different direction and I’m at a cukiernia (pastry shop) for some of the best pączki (doughnuts) I’ve ever had. Next door, a kawiarnia (coffee shop) where one of the baristas, Vera, helps me with my Polish and makes me feel like I’m welcome in Poland.

Which, like, okay, I can’t emphasize enough just how important meeting people like Vera has been. Because, despite how quickly so many things have solidified as normal aspects of everyday life. Despite how much simpler it was than we thought it would be to not only find an apartment, but get bank accounts, and cell service, and our PESEL numbers, and even our residency cards. Despite how much we’ve been enjoying our experience living in Europe, there’s still stuff that was and continues to be difficult for me. And one of those things is the mental weight of feeling like a burden almost anywhere I go.

The reality is that, even though many, many people in Poland speak English, the language barrier is still very real and very much a barrier, even if it’s mostly psychological on my end. Ironically, I’ve perfected saying, “Sorry, I don’t speak Polish well.” *Przepraszam, nie mówię dobrze po polsku.* I essentially lead with it in every interaction, because the second I get a follow-up question and freeze because I don’t understand, I just feel so. Fucking. Stupid. The first couple months, especially, I had to psyche myself up to just go into shops, because while I obviously want to practice my Polish and become more comfortable being uncomfortable, I’m also so very tired. As though I’ve hoisted the weight of my entire existence upon my shoulders and carry it with me everywhere I go. Just thinking about stepping into the bakery and asking for a slice of pizza becomes this unanticipated slog through self-consciousness. I feel so fucking stupid almost every single day, and the days I don’t feel so fucking stupid are the days where I don’t leave the apartment.

Almost every single day I am reminded how much of my life I’ve lived in service of making myself small. To stay out of the way. To not become someone else’s problem by merely being present. Almost every single day I’m confronted with the feeling that I’ve failed at standing up to that looming specter of doubt. The voice telling me that everyone, me included, is better off if I am not noticed. The last few years of my life, where I’ve deliberately challenged myself to live in defiance of that notion, now feels like it was all a waste because I have to consciously decide how much discomfort I can bear when I want to try zapiekanka (I dunno, google it), and more often than not I choose to go hungry instead.[^3] Every potential interaction is first played out in my head, where I assess if my mental battery is charged enough to handle being so vulnerable, so naked, so conspicuous. And it often isn’t. Everywhere I go, any time I interact with anyone, I am no longer able to minimize myself. I’m the foreigner. I’m babbling poorly-formed Polish, apologizing, and hoping they understand, or I fall back to English and hope they understand that instead.

I feel so lonely. Lonely because I don’t belong. Lonely because I can’t express myself. Lonely because almost everyone I love is at least six hours behind me each day. By the time most of my friends are waking up, my day is winding down. The entire dynamic of our relationship has changed. We communicate in short bursts of text messages in the sliver of consciousness we share.

There have been so many days where I don’t know how anyone does this. How anyone moves to a new country where they don’t speak the language or fully appreciate the immensity of the centuries of history that shaped it into what it is now. How anyone says goodbye to the people they love and chooses a life of isolation. And I’m left in awe at the strength of anyone who tries.

And then there are so many days where the awe I feel is at how much I love it here. Where my wife, our dog, and I can hop on a train and find ourselves exploring Gdańsk in less than three hours. Where we can walk through some of the biggest, most gorgeous parks we’ve ever seen. Where my friend says that he’ll be in Vienna in February and I can get there by train for, like, $100 to meet him. Where we’ll have fresh bread and croissants and butter and jam for breakfast. Where we’re in a place where it feels like people enjoying *existing*. People having picnics in the park. People eating ice cream. People at the mall. Holy shit, malls! Like, actual malls with stores and services that don’t feel like fucking ghost towns like they did back in the US. Third spaces. People living. It’s what I’ve dreamed a city could be.[^4]

Four months. I’ve lived here four months. Holy shit, it’s been four months. Holy shit, it’s only been four months. It feels like it’s been four days. It feels like it’s been four years. That’s how time works now. Yesterday, I was driving through the mountains in Tennessee on my way to Florida. Today, I said *Przepraszam, nie mówię dobrze po polsku* six times.[^5] Tomorrow, I’ll feel just a little bit better. And tomorrow, I’ll feel just a little bit worse. And tomorrow, I’ll look at my wife and say, “I can’t believe we live here” for the five-hundredth time.

I can’t believe we live here. 

I did my best to envision what life in Poland would be like, but there was no way to truly prepare. I had no idea how stunning, how green, how walkable it would be. Warsaw is an incredible city full of beautiful parks and impeccable transit and astounding food. A city that feels modern and historic. A city full of people who are kind and patient. People like Vera at the kawiarnia, and Aga and Dawid at Labuna, and Katarzyna at the Italian cafe down the street, and Piotr at Błysk, one of the most incredible coffee shops I’ve had the pleasure of experiencing. And then there are people like my new friends Paweł and Paulina, or Kasia, Kinga, and Donata[^6], who have made me not only feel welcome, but like I am going to manage to build a community here with my wife. That despite everything, each day is going to get easier. Because I have my family. I have friends. I have a place.
